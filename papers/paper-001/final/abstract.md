This bounded critical literature review examines how self-consistency checking, separated verification, and evidence-based fact-checking address hallucinations in text generated by large language models and what limitations remain. It compares published findings on checking mechanisms, evaluation outcomes, and methodological constraints, distinguishing factual truth from agreement among responses and support in specified evidence. Consistency checking can aid detection, but repeated errors undermine confidence in stable outputs. Separating verification questions from an initial response can improve performance in particular settings, while consulting another model does not guarantee independent knowledge or correctness. Documentary fact-checking provides inspectable support and can improve attribution, yet its value depends on source quality, evidence completeness, and interpretation; missing support does not establish falsity. Detection, correction, and improved attribution are distinct outcomes, and precision gains require consideration of abstention, response coverage, and retained useful information. Differences in tasks, metrics, models, and evaluation designs limit direct comparison and generalization. The review supports conditional use of these approaches rather than a universal ranking or guarantee of truth. Matched evaluations should assess common outcomes, held-out human judgments, coverage, source reliability, and actual resource costs.
